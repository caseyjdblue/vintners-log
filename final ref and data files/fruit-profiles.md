# Fruit Profiles — Winemaking Reference Catalogue

**Catalogue:** Fruit Profiles (Reference File 01)
**Climate basis:** Pacific Northwest, western Washington (USDA zones 8a–8b, maritime climate)
**Scope of this edition:** Sweet cherries, strawberries, blueberries, apples (hard cider), blackberries
**Status:** Living document — add fruits and refine numbers from your own batch logs over time.

---

## How to use this file

Each fruit follows the same template so profiles stay comparable across the catalogue and cross-reference cleanly into the Recipes, Yeast Strains, and Additives catalogues as those are built. Numbers given are typical starting points for fruit grown or foraged in western Washington; always confirm against your own measurements, since variety, ripeness, and season shift them.

### Glossary / conventions

- **Brix (°Bx):** Sugar content of the fresh fruit, as % sugar by weight. Rough guide to natural fermentable sugar before any sugar is added.
- **SG / OG / FG:** Specific gravity / original (starting) gravity / final gravity, read with a hydrometer. Water = 1.000.
- **TA:** Titratable acidity, expressed as % tartaric acid. Most balanced fruit wines land around 0.55–0.70%.
- **pH:** Affects flavor balance, microbial stability, and sulfite effectiveness. Wine target is roughly 3.2–3.6.
- **ABV:** Alcohol by volume. Estimate as `ABV ≈ (OG − FG) × 131.25`.
- **Chaptalization:** Adding sugar to the must to reach a target OG. Rule of thumb: ~1 lb cane sugar in 1 gallon raises SG by ~0.045. Always add sugar gradually and confirm with the hydrometer rather than by recipe alone.
- **Fruit ratio:** Pounds of fruit per finished gallon. Higher ratio = more flavor, color, and body.
- **Must:** The unfermented mix of fruit, water, sugar, and additives.

### Standard additive shorthand (detailed in the Additives catalogue)

- **Campden / K-meta:** Potassium metabisulfite. ~1 crushed tablet (or 1/16 tsp powder) per gallon to sanitize the must; wait 12–24 h before pitching yeast. Also used at bottling to protect from oxidation.
- **Pectic enzyme:** Breaks down pectin to improve juice yield, color extraction, and clarity. Nearly all of these fruits are pectin-rich and benefit from it.
- **Yeast nutrient / energizer:** Fruit musts are nutrient-poor compared to grapes; nutrient prevents stuck/sluggish fermentation and off-aromas (H₂S).
- **Acid blend:** Raises TA when a fruit is low-acid (notably cherries).
- **Wine tannin (e.g. FT Rouge / grape tannin):** Adds structure and mouthfeel to low-tannin fruits.
- **Potassium sorbate:** Stabilizer added with sulfite *before back-sweetening* to prevent refermentation. Never add to an actively fermenting wine.

### Core equipment assumed

Hydrometer + test jar, acid titration kit (or pH meter), fermenting vessel with airlock, straining bag, racking cane/auto-siphon, sanitizer.

## Sweet Cherry

### Overview
Makes a rich, deep-colored wine that can run from an off-dry table style to a port-like dessert wine. Naturally high in sugar but **low in acid**, so cherry musts usually need acid added to avoid a flat, flabby finish.

### Recommended varieties (western WA)
Bing (deep color, classic flavor), Rainier (sweeter, lighter color — often blended), Lapins, Sweetheart, Skeena. Tart/pie cherries (Montmorency) can be blended in for acid and brightness if available.

### Harvest window
Late June through July; peak early-to-mid July in lowland western WA. A short, fast season — plan ahead and freeze fruit if you can't ferment immediately (freezing also helps cell breakdown).

### Fruit characteristics
- Brix: 14–20 (one of the higher-sugar fruits here)
- TA: low, ~0.45–0.60% — **plan to add acid blend**
- pH: 3.7–4.0 (high; watch microbial stability, dose sulfite accordingly)
- Tannin: moderate from skins; pits add structure but also bitter almond/benzaldehyde notes if cracked
- Pectin: moderate–high — use pectic enzyme
- Color: excellent, deep ruby (Bing); fairly stable

### Winemaking parameters
- Fruit ratio: 3–4 lb/gal
- Target OG: ~1.085–1.095 (table); 1.110+ for high-ABV dessert/port style
- Target ABV: 11–12% table; up to ~18% for port style
- Recommended yeast: **Lalvin 71B** (softens acid, fruit-forward) or **RC212** (red structure); **EC-1118** for high-ABV dessert style
- Key additives: Campden, pectic enzyme, acid blend (to ~0.60% TA), yeast nutrient; optional wine tannin

### Featured recipe — Sweet Cherry Table Wine (per 1 gal)
- 3.5 lb sweet cherries (pitted; reserve a handful of pits whole if you want subtle almond depth — do **not** crush them)
- ~1.75–2 lb cane sugar (add gradually to reach OG ~1.090)
- Water to 1 gallon
- 1 crushed Campden tablet
- 1/2 tsp pectic enzyme
- ~1–1.5 tsp acid blend (to ~0.60% TA — adjust to measurement)
- 1 tsp yeast nutrient
- 71B yeast

### Fermentation process & timeline
1. Pit and lightly crush cherries in a straining bag; add to vessel with water and dissolved sugar.
2. Add Campden + pectic enzyme; cover and wait 12–24 h.
3. Pitch yeast + half the nutrient. Primary (on the fruit) 5–7 days at 65–72°F, punching the cap down daily. Add the remaining nutrient at the 1/3 sugar break.
4. Press/strain out fruit, rack to a carboy with airlock for secondary.
5. Ferment to dry (FG ~0.995); rack off lees at ~3 weeks, again at 2–3 months.

### Aging & drinking window
Needs time. Minimum 6–12 months; noticeably better at 1 year+ as tannin and color integrate. Stable for 2–3 years.

### Tips / watch-outs
- Don't crush the pits — small amounts whole are fine for flavor, but crushed pits release benzaldehyde and trace cyanogenic compounds.
- Because acid is low and pH high, be diligent with sanitation and sulfite.
- Tempting to over-sugar; keep table versions balanced or the wine turns syrupy.

## Strawberry

### Overview
Delicate, aromatic, and notoriously tricky: low sugar, low tannin, and a color/aroma that fade fast. The goal is a light, fresh, often off-dry wine meant to be enjoyed **young**. Frequently blended with rhubarb for body and acid.

### Recommended varieties (western WA)
Hood and Shuksan (classic PNW June-bearers, intense flavor, soft — ideal for wine), Totem, Albion or Seascape (day-neutral/everbearing for a longer harvest).

### Harvest window
June-bearers peak in June (late May–June). Everbearing/day-neutral types crop from June through first frost (into October). Use fully ripe, deep-red fruit — flavor is everything here.

### Fruit characteristics
- Brix: 7–9 (low — significant sugar addition required)
- TA: moderate–high, ~0.6–0.9%
- pH: 3.3–3.5
- Tannin: very low (consider a light tannin addition for structure)
- Pectin: high — pectic enzyme essential, or expect a permanent haze
- Color: poor stability — oxidizes from red toward orange/brown quickly

### Winemaking parameters
- Fruit ratio: 3–4 lb/gal (flavor is delicate; don't skimp)
- Target OG: ~1.080–1.085 (keep ABV modest so fruit isn't buried)
- Target ABV: 10–11%
- Recommended yeast: **Lalvin QA23** or **Côte des Blancs** — cool, aroma-preserving ferment that protects delicate strawberry character; **71B** as a fruit-forward alternative
- Key additives: Campden, pectic enzyme, yeast nutrient; optional light wine tannin

### Featured recipe — Light Strawberry Wine (per 1 gal)
- 3.5–4 lb ripe strawberries (hulled, mashed in a straining bag)
- ~1.75 lb cane sugar (to OG ~1.082)
- Water to 1 gallon
- 1 crushed Campden tablet
- 1/2 tsp pectic enzyme
- 1 tsp yeast nutrient
- Optional: 1/4 tsp wine tannin
- QA23 yeast (or Côte des Blancs)

### Fermentation process & timeline
1. Mash berries in bag; combine with water + dissolved sugar.
2. Campden + pectic enzyme; wait 12–24 h.
3. Pitch yeast + half the nutrient. **Ferment cool (60–68°F)** to preserve aroma. Primary 3–5 days on the fruit. Add the remaining nutrient at the 1/3 sugar break.
4. Strain/press; rack to carboy. Secondary to dry over ~2 weeks.
5. Rack early and protect from air — minimize headspace and oxygen exposure.

### Aging & drinking window
**Drink young — 3–6 months**, ideally within a year. Strawberry wine does *not* improve with long aging; color and aroma degrade. This is the opposite of cherry/blackberry.

### Tips / watch-outs
- Oxygen and light are the enemy: top up carboys, bottle promptly, store dark and cool.
- If color/flavor loss is a concern, blend ~25% rhubarb or a splash of red fruit (raspberry) for stability.
- Back-sweetening slightly (with sorbate + sulfite first) suits the fresh style.

## Blueberry

### Overview
One of the best fruits for a "red-wine-style" country wine — deep color, good body, and enough structure to age. Western WA is prime blueberry country, so fruit is abundant and cheap in season.

### Recommended varieties (western WA)
Highbush types do well here: Duke, Bluecrop, Draper, Liberty, Elliott (late). A mix of early/mid/late varieties spreads the harvest and balances sugar and acid.

### Harvest window
July through September; peak August. Freezing the berries before fermenting ruptures cells and dramatically improves juice and color release.

### Fruit characteristics
- Brix: 10–15
- TA: moderate, ~0.5–0.8%
- pH: 3.2–3.4
- Tannin: low–moderate (skins contribute)
- Pectin: high — pectic enzyme essential
- Color: excellent, deep purple, and stable

### Winemaking parameters
- Fruit ratio: 2–3 lb/gal (strong flavor; less fruit needed than the berries below)
- Target OG: ~1.085–1.095
- Target ABV: 11–12%
- Recommended yeast: **RC212** or **BM4x4** (red fruit structure), **71B** for a softer, fruit-forward result
- Key additives: Campden, pectic enzyme, yeast nutrient; optional tannin

### Featured recipe — Blueberry Red-Style Wine (per 1 gal)
- 2.5–3 lb blueberries (frozen then thawed, lightly crushed in a bag)
- ~2 lb cane sugar (to OG ~1.090)
- Water to 1 gallon
- 1 crushed Campden tablet
- 1/2 tsp pectic enzyme
- 1 tsp yeast nutrient
- Optional: 1/4 tsp wine tannin
- RC212 yeast (rehydrated)

### Fermentation process & timeline
1. Freeze/thaw and crush berries in bag; combine with water + dissolved sugar.
2. Campden + pectic enzyme; wait 12–24 h.
3. Pitch yeast + half the nutrient. Primary on the fruit 5–7 days at 68–74°F, punching the cap daily for color. Add the remaining nutrient at the 1/3 sugar break (especially important for RC212/BM4x4).
4. Press/strain; rack to carboy for secondary; ferment to dry.
5. Rack at ~3 weeks and again at 2–3 months; bulk age.

### Aging & drinking window
6–12 months minimum; rewards 1–2 years. Tannin and color hold up well, so this is a good candidate for longer bulk aging.

### Tips / watch-outs
- Freeze the fruit first — single biggest quality lever for blueberry.
- Watch for stuck ferments if nutrient is skipped.

## Apple (Hard Cider)

### Overview
Different process from the berry/stone-fruit wines: ferment **fresh-pressed juice with no added water** (and usually little or no added sugar). Balance comes from blending apple types — sweet, sharp (acid), and bittersweet/bittersharp (tannin).

### Recommended varieties (western WA)
- Sweet/base: Gala, Fuji, Jonagold, Honeycrisp
- Sharp (acid): Granny Smith, Bramley
- Bittersweet/tannin: Kingston Black (a standout single-variety cider apple grown in the PNW), Yarlington Mill, or a handful of crabapples for tannin and aroma
A workable blend is roughly 50% sweet / 35% sharp / 15% bittersweet.

### Harvest window
September through November; most cider/late dessert apples ripen October–November. Fresh juice is the goal, so press soon after harvest (or buy fresh, unpasteurized juice with no preservatives — preservatives like potassium sorbate will block fermentation).

### Fruit characteristics
- Brix: 11–15 (juice OG typically ~1.045–1.060 with no sugar added)
- TA: variety-dependent — that's why you blend
- pH: 3.3–3.8
- Tannin: from bittersweets/crabs — gives mouthfeel and ageability
- Pectin: present; pectic enzyme optional (some leave it for body, others add it for clarity)

### Winemaking parameters
- Juice only — no water, little/no sugar
- Target OG: ~1.050–1.060 (add a little sugar only if pushing toward 7–8%)
- Target ABV: 5–8% (session 5–6%; stronger 7–8%). For a 10–12% "apple wine," chaptalize — but that's a different, more wine-like product.
- Recommended yeast: cider-specific (Mangrove Jack's M02, White Labs WLP775) for fruitiness; **EC-1118**/champagne for dry and crisp; **71B** for a softer, fruit-forward cider; ale yeast for fuller body
- Key additives: Campden (unless using pasteurized juice and pitching immediately), yeast nutrient; pectic enzyme optional

### Featured recipe — Traditional Dry Hard Cider (per 1 gal)
- 1 gallon fresh-pressed, preservative-free apple juice (blended varieties as above), OG ~1.050
- 1 crushed Campden tablet (skip if juice is already pasteurized and you pitch right away)
- 1 tsp yeast nutrient
- Optional: 1/4 tsp pectic enzyme for clarity
- Cider or EC-1118 yeast

### Fermentation process & timeline
1. Add Campden to fresh juice; wait 24 h (skip for pasteurized juice).
2. Pitch yeast + half the nutrient. Primary 1–2 weeks at 60–68°F for M02/EC-1118 (cooler = cleaner, fruitier); ferment **WLP775 warmer, ~68–72°F**, to stay in its range. Add the remaining nutrient at the 1/3 sugar break.
3. Ferment to dry (FG ~0.998–1.000); rack off lees.
4. **Carbonation choice:** leave still, *or* bottle-condition (prime with a small measured sugar dose) for sparkle, *or* force-carbonate in keg.
5. Back-sweeten only with sorbate + sulfite added first (note: sorbate blocks bottle-conditioning — choose one or the other).

### Aging & drinking window
Drinkable younger than the fruit wines — 1–3 months — but smooths and improves over 6–12 months. Bittersweet-heavy blends age longest.

### Tips / watch-outs
- Confirm juice has **no potassium sorbate/benzoate** or it won't ferment.
- Don't bottle-condition *and* sorbate — pick sparkling-via-priming or stabilized-and-sweetened.
- Keep ferment cool for a cleaner, more aromatic cider.

## Blackberry

### Overview
Abundant and free in western WA thanks to the invasive Himalayan blackberry — excellent foraging fruit. Makes a robust, deeply colored red-style wine with real tannin structure; also great for port-style. One of the most reliable and rewarding country wines.

### Recommended varieties (western WA)
Himalayan (foraged, ubiquitous, ripens reliably), trailing/cultivated types like Marionberry and Boysenberry (richer, more complex — worth blending in), Triple Crown (thornless cultivated).

### Harvest window
Late July through September; Himalayan peaks August–September. Pick fully black and slightly soft; underripe berries are sharp and overly astringent.

### Fruit characteristics
- Brix: 8–12
- TA: moderate–high, ~0.8–1.2% (bright acidity)
- pH: 3.0–3.4
- Tannin: moderate–high from seeds and skins — good structure, but can be astringent
- Pectin: high — pectic enzyme essential
- Color: deep, rich, and stable

### Winemaking parameters
- Fruit ratio: 3–4 lb/gal
- Target OG: ~1.085–1.095
- Target ABV: 11–13%
- Recommended yeast: **RC212** or **Pasteur Red** (structure), **71B** (softer, fruit-forward), **EC-1118** for port-style
- Key additives: Campden, pectic enzyme, yeast nutrient; acid usually fine as-is — measure before adjusting

### Featured recipe — Blackberry Red-Style Wine (per 1 gal)
- 3.5–4 lb blackberries (foraged or cultivated; freeze/thaw, then crush in a straining bag — do **not** crush the seeds)
- ~2 lb cane sugar (to OG ~1.090)
- Water to 1 gallon
- 1 crushed Campden tablet
- 1/2 tsp pectic enzyme
- 1 tsp yeast nutrient
- RC212 yeast (rehydrated)

### Fermentation process & timeline
1. Freeze/thaw and crush berries in bag (gentle — keep seeds intact); combine with water + dissolved sugar.
2. Campden + pectic enzyme; wait 12–24 h.
3. Pitch yeast + half the nutrient. Primary on the fruit 5–7 days at 68–74°F, punching the cap daily. Add the remaining nutrient at the 1/3 sugar break (especially important for RC212).
4. Press/strain gently; rack to carboy; ferment to dry.
5. Rack at ~3 weeks and again at 2–3 months; bulk age.

### Aging & drinking window
6–12 months minimum; clearly better at 1–2 years as tannin softens. Excellent long-aging fruit and a strong port-style candidate.

### Tips / watch-outs
- Don't crush the seeds — they add harsh bitterness.
- Strain thoroughly; fine seed sediment is persistent.
- High tannin + bright acid can read austere when young — give it time, or back-sweeten slightly for an approachable style.

## General notes & cross-references

- **Sanitation first.** Most fruit-wine faults trace back to wild yeast/bacteria or oxidation. Sanitize everything; use Campden and sulfite as scheduled.
- **Measure, don't guess.** Variety, ripeness, and season swing Brix and acid widely. Take an OG reading and a TA/pH reading on every batch and adjust to target — the recipe amounts are starting points.
- **Nutrient matters.** These fruits are nutrient-poor vs. grapes; skipping nutrient is the top cause of stuck ferments and sulfur off-aromas.
- **Stabilize before sweetening.** Any back-sweetened wine needs potassium sorbate + sulfite added *before* the sugar, or it can referment in the bottle (the one exception being bottle-conditioned sparkling cider, which relies on refermentation).
